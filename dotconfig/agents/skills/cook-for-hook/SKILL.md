# Cook for Hook

Turn one clear idea into an arresting, defensible hook. Improve the **idea before the wording**.

Based on Luke Iha's four-step process in [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=or1z2n3QfiQ): **isolate → commit → deep fry → make it bang**. The original context is advertising; the naming and output conventions below adapt the method for agent use.

## Establish the brief

Use the conversation to identify the audience, desired reaction, medium, underlying material, and hard constraints. Ask only for missing information that would materially change the work; otherwise state a useful assumption and proceed.

When working from supplied material, read it fully before mining it. Distinguish exact source wording from your own adaptations. For revisions, identify what the current hook is trying to say before rewriting it.

**Ready when:** you can name the reader, the intended effect, and the format's constraints.

## 1. Isolate the idea

Write the point in a single sentence of plain, almost caveman language. Strip away the hook, cleverness, setup, mechanism, and offer until you can answer: **What am I actually trying to say?**

Judge that underlying idea from the reader's perspective:

- What do they already believe about this?
- What would surprise them, contradict an assumption, or matter to them?
- What changes for them if this is true?

An obvious observation needs a stronger angle, not louder adjectives. If the material contains several ideas, separate them into distinct possible briefs.

**Done when:** one sentence expresses one point, and you can identify its reader-relevant tension or consequence.

## 2. Commit to the idea

Choose the point this batch will live or die by. Keep its supporting mechanism and evidence available, but let every candidate express that same point.

Be **monogamous to the idea**: improve its expression instead of rescuing it with extra benefits, topics, or promises. If exploring multiple concepts is requested, run a separate batch for each.

**Done when:** each candidate can be traced to the same plain-language sentence without adding another thesis.

## 3. Deep fry the idea

Take the committed idea to its most bizarre, extended, emotionally stimulating expression, then walk it back to a version you can land truthfully.

### Explore before polishing

Use **associative, squishy logic**: ask “What is this like?” repeatedly. Explore images, identities, roles, consequences, and familiar situations. Start from the audience's psychology—what they desire, fear, resent, aspire to, or find surprising—rather than merely listing features or technical facts.

For an open-ended brief, draft at least eight rough directions across at least four moves below. These counts are working defaults, not rules from the video; scale to the user's requested scope. Different synonyms for the same sentence count as one direction.

| Move | Question to cook with |
| --- | --- |
| Good → bad | When does the apparent advantage become a liability? |
| Bad → good | When does the apparent flaw become an advantage? |
| Ineffective or neutral → harmful | Could this actually make the problem worse, rather than merely fail to help? |
| Identity contrast | What unexpected behavior or result clashes with who someone seems to be? |
| Reverse causation | Might the apparent effect help produce the apparent cause? |
| Types of types | What specific subtype or condition changes the familiar story? |
| Rename the phenomenon | What vivid name makes this pattern recognizable and memorable? |

Treat these as prompts for exploration, not permission to invent facts. Pursue **contrast, surprise, nuance, and stakes**; choose the combination that strengthens this particular idea.

### Land the tightrope

The **tightrope effect** is the reader wondering, “How can you possibly justify that?” The next beat must deliver a satisfying explanation.

For each promising direction, identify:

1. The arresting claim or image.
2. Its thread back to the original idea and available evidence.
3. The next sentence or explanation that makes it land.

Preserve the truth while stretching its expression. Keep essential conditions in the hook when omitting them would mislead. A metaphor should illuminate the mechanism; an invented phenomenon name should remain identifiable as your framing. Discard directions that require invented evidence or a bait-and-switch explanation.

**Done when:** you have distinct directions, and every surviving one has a credible landing that preserves the committed idea.

## 4. Make it bang

Only now optimize the words:

- **Prime real estate first:** move the arresting claim, contrast, or consequence toward the beginning.
- **Specific, not overloaded:** use the concrete detail that makes the idea real; remove details that delay comprehension.
- **Active language:** make someone or something act. Dramatize what changes, what it costs, or what collides.
- **One to three emotional trigger words:** choose words that carry the actual stakes. The transcript's clear examples include *rot, panic, poison, strips, drowning, mutates,* and *yanked*. Fit the register and audience; intensity is not synonymous with profanity.
- **Compression:** cut throat-clearing, repeated meaning, and explanatory clauses that belong in the next beat.

Read each finalist aloud. Reject any whose cleverness obscures the idea. A useful plain hook beats a strained outrageous one.

**Done when:** the strongest words arrive early, every finalist is immediately intelligible, and its promise can be fulfilled by the material that follows.

## Adapt to the deliverable

### Names, usernames, and taglines

Treat the name as a compressed hook and the profile or surrounding copy as its landing. Prefer a name that remains interpretable without a lengthy explanation. Preserve whether the user wants a person, a publication, or a product to be evoked.

Verify character counts and permitted characters against the requested platform before presenting finalists. State availability only if actually checked. Evaluate memorability and spoken readability alongside emotional force.

### Existing winners and format-led hooks

The video's closing exception: not every hook needs reinvention. When the user supplies a proven winner, preserve its working structure as a control and offer variations separately. Describe performance as proven only when supporting results are available.

For a requested format—such as a timeline, “this is what happens when…”, or personification—let that format organize the hook before adding novelty.

## Deliver

Default to the strongest recommendation first, followed by a short, ranked shortlist of meaningfully different alternatives. Unless the user requests another shape, include:

- The committed idea in one plain sentence.
- Five polished options, with a brief reason or tradeoff for each.
- A landing sentence for the leading hook when writing opening copy.
- Verified lengths when the format imposes a character limit.

Keep the rough creative exploration out of the final answer unless requested. Rank by fidelity to the idea, audience relevance, arresting tension, clarity, and ability to deliver—not by shock alone. Treat these as editorial judgments, not predictions of measured performance.
